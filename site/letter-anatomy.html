Chapter 3 — Complaint Letter Anatomy

One sentence to remember: A complaint letter is a request with receipts, not a speech.

The 10-second frame

Every effective complaint letter has exactly four jobs:

  1. Tell them who you are and what happened (one sentence, with date + ref number).
  2. Tell them why they should fix it (the promise they made).
  3. Tell them what you want (one specific remedy).
  4. Tell them when you need a reply (a real date), and politely hint at the next rung of the ladder.

Everything else — your feelings, your backstory, the 12 previous phone calls in detail — is optional, and usually a liability.

The anatomy, top to bottom

1. Subject line that does the work Not "Complaint!!!" — instead:

Subject: Refund request — Order #38122 — Ref #ABC-4192 — reply requested by Nov 21

Put your reference numbers in the subject. Gatekeepers route by subject lines.

2. One-sentence summary (the "headline paragraph")

"I paid $189 for a Model X-200 coffee maker on March 14 (receipt attached); it stopped working on March 21, and your store declined the refund on March 28."

If the whole tone of the letter is firm and the first sentence carries every key fact, the letter is already 80% done. Busy readers often read only this.

3. The short body — facts first Three to five numbered paragraphs or bullet lines: - What you bought/received, amount, date. - What went wrong, with a photo/document reference ("see attached photo 2, showing the cracked housing"). - What the company's own policy says (warranty text, return window, policy clause) — quote it briefly and name it.

4. The ask — one sentence, one number

"I'm asking for a full refund of $189 to the original payment method."

Not "reasonable compensation," not "what I'm owed," not a 6-paragraph narrative about how it made you feel. One remedy, one number.

5. The deadline — a real date

"Please respond by Friday, March 19, 2026 (14 days from the date of this letter)."

Real dates beat "ASAP" — "ASAP" means nothing once it's on anyone's calendar. ~14 days is a fair, firm window for a first letter.

6. The polite next step (the escalation hint)

"If I don't hear back by then, I'll take the next step and file a complaint with the consumer protection office — I'd rather resolve it directly with you first."

That's the whole threat, delivered calmly. It's not empty (Chapter 2 shows the rung you'll file with), and it's not rude.

7. Attachments list Numbered: "1. Receipt. 2. Photos 1–3. 3. Call log with ref #'s." Referencing items by number in the body makes them easy to verify and hard to ignore.

8. Closing + contact block Your name, phone or email (whichever you'll actually respond on), and your full mailing address if there's any chance this becomes formal. Keep the sign-off warm and short: "Thank you — I do want to sort this out with you first. — Dana"

What to cut (the famous "tone killers")

  • ❌ The word "furious," "ridiculous," "incompetent," or "scam" anywhere. A letter that calls the reader names gets forwarded to a discard folder, not a decision-maker.
  • ❌ Caps lock and triple exclamation marks.
  • ❌ Long stories about how much you've been a loyal customer — one sentence is plenty.
  • ❌ Threats you can't/won't actually take ("I'll take you to the media" — don't say it unless it's true and you've thought it through).
  • ❌ Legal-sounding language you don't understand. It reads as posturing, and it can make real trouble later.
  • ❌ More than one page. One page. Really.

What to add for tone (polite persistence)

  • ✅ Appreciation that's plausible: "I've been a customer for 4 years; I'd like to resolve this directly with you first."
  • ✅ Collaboration verbs: "sort out," "resolve," "confirm." Not "demand," "insist."
  • ✅ Your paper trail, summarized as facts: "I've logged my calls and kept my receipts and photos, and happy to share whatever's needed."

The one-page template (copy this)

Date:           2026-03-04
To:             <company — complaints desk / specific person if you have it>
From:           <your name>, <your town>
Re:             <issue — 1 line> — Ref #ABC-4192 — reply by 2026-03-18

Dear <name or Sir/Madam>,

<One-sentence headline.>

Facts:
  1. <What happened — fact, date, amount>
  2. <What went wrong — cite attachment #>
  3. <Their promise/policy that wasn't met>

The ask:
  <One remedy, one number.>

The deadline:
  Please reply by <date> (14 days from this letter).

The next step:
  If I haven't heard back, I'll escalate to <regulator/rung> — though I'd
  much prefer to resolve it with you directly.

Attachments: 1) receipt 2) photos 3) call log
Contact: <phone/email>

Thank you — I do want to sort this out with you first.

<Your name>

That's the whole thing. Mine it, adapt it, keep it to one page. Then pair it with the ready-made letters in the Everyday Letters Pack.

The send rules

  • In writing, always. Verbal requests evaporate; letters are the spine of the ladder.
  • Between the letter and the post: email first (cheap, timestamped), and mail a printed copy for anything high-stakes — delivery confirmation costs a couple of dollars and pays for itself.
  • Keep a copy of exactly what you sent in 04-letters-sent/. When someone says "we never received a response from you," you'll be holding the good copy.
  • Time-stamp your sent mail. A printed email with its Date header counted in 1 dispute is worth ten recollections.
