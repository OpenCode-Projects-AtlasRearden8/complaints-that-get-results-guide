Chapter 5 — Small Claims Basics

One sentence to remember: Small claims court is the no-lawyers courthouse for ordinary people — and for most of us it's a last resort we'll never actually need.

What small claims court actually is

In the US, every state runs some version of a "small claims" division or docket. In other countries it's a county court track or a tribunal. The features that matter to a beginner are nearly universal:

  • No lawyers needed (some places disallow them, some allow but don't expect them).
  • Small dollar caps — typically ranging from a few thousand dollars up to $10,000 or $25,000, depending on your state or region. Your amount matters here: if your claim is bigger than the cap, small claims can't be the whole answer.
  • Cheap filing — commonly $20–$100, and some places allow a fee waiver.
  • Fast and informal — months, not years; sometimes with online filing and remote hearings.
  • Businesses can be asked for the remedy, but the "hearing" is a conversation, not a courtroom drama.

The five things to know before you file

1. Confirm the fit. The right disputes: unpaid deposits, unrepaired damage caused by the other side, goods paid for and never delivered, work not done or done badly, refund refused after a reasonable ask. Wrong fit: complex contracts, big-dollar claims, anything with a legal technicality buried inside.

2. Find the right court/office. Small claims venues are local — a neighborhood-level office in the US; a county court or tribunal elsewhere. The county (or region) is typically where the other party lives or where the event happened. The court clerk's office (or website) tells you exactly how it works there — and this is a place where asking the clerk a procedural question is expected, not a favor.

3. Prepare your packet. This is Chapter 1, paying off directly: - A copy of your entire timeline, printed. - The receipt/invoice/contract, printed. (And an extra copy — the judge wants one and each other side usually needs to be "served" a full set.) - Two labeled copies of your three best pieces of evidence. Three best > thirty marginal. - Two blank pages and a working pen — you'll take notes when it's the other side's turn.

4. Understand what you're asking for. You state a specific amount and why: "I'm seeking $189 for goods paid but not delivered on <date>." Damages and remedies are measured in money. Agreeing on "what you want" before you walk in — a specific amount — is a legal requirement and 90% of the work anyway.

5. Know the name on the filing. You generally sue the business owner, not the clerk at the counter — the legal owner may be an LLC name you've never heard of. Your county's small claims help desk or website explains how to find the registered business name (a business-license search or a tax lookup, both usually free and online).

A typical process, start to finish

1. Final letter (Chapter 3) → 3 weeks' wait → ask once more.
2. Confirm the venue + dollar cap + gather documents.
3. File the claim (small define format: amount, reason, name/address of other party).
4. "Serve" the other side properly — the court sets HOW; do it right, it's
   required and bailiff-copying this is easy to botch.
5. Wait — set aside a half-day for the hearing date.
6. Hearing day: bring three copies of your packet, your log, and your receipts.
7. The other side may settle at the courthouse door the morning of.
8. If you win: collect what you're owed (see below), not automatically — you
   may need to ask for a payment plan or a debt-levy step.

Step 8 surprises people most: winning is one thing; collecting is another. The judge can tell you what you're owed; getting paid is a separate step the clerk's office can direct (payment plan requests, wage/revenue-withholding, etc.). In practice, many businesses just pay after the ruling letter arrives.

Before you file, ask these questions

  • Is the amount worth my time? (Include filing + travel + your hours.)
  • Have I genuinely tried the regulator/ombudsman route? Some disputes land faster there, cost less, and demand less of you.
  • Is the other party actually collectible? If they have no money, most outcomes don't help. A ruling against a struggling landlord may be hollow; against a national chain it's a fix that ripples.
  • Is a payment plan what I actually want?

What this chapter does not do

This is a tour of the landscape — not a do-it-yourself court kit, and it's not legal advice. Rules (forms, service, caps, fees, deadlines) differ by state/county/country, and by claim type. Before you file anything, do these two things:

  1. Read your own small claims office's own pages. They are usually written for first-timers like you, often with videos. (Search: <your county/city> small claims clerk.)
  2. Ask the clerk one question. "I've never done this; is there a first-timer checklist?" Costs nothing. Courts exist to run these dockets; asking is normal.

And if the amount, the claim, or what happened is complicated — a consultation with a legal-aid office / document review service is cheaper than re-filing a botched case.
